The intersection

Clinical AI has a well-documented weak point that sits upstream of the modelling: the quality, provenance and governance of the data it learns from. Label noise, unrepresentative cohorts, consent that doesn't cover secondary use, and evaluation against a flawed reference standard are not engineering problems. They are clinical and governance problems.

My work sits on that side of the line. I am not the person who trains the model. I am the person who defines what "correct" means, who can say whether a dataset reflects real disease presentation, and who has spent four years building evidence systems that stand up to external audit.

Machine learning: what I actually contribute

I collaborate on AI research in oncology as the clinical domain expert. Stated plainly: the modelling is done by computational collaborators; the clinical validity is mine. That contribution covers:

  • Ground truth definition: establishing the diagnostic reference standard a model is trained and judged against, and being explicit about where that standard is itself uncertain
  • Annotation and labelling: histopathological interpretation converted into structured labels, with attention to inter-observer variability rather than pretending it away
  • Cohort and case selection: whether a dataset represents the spectrum of disease actually seen in practice, or only its unambiguous extremes
  • Failure-mode review: reading model errors clinically: which misclassifications are tolerable, which would change management, which would harm
  • Clinical plausibility of features: distinguishing a signal that reflects biology from one that reflects a staining artefact or a scanner
  • Reporting rigour: study design and manuscript work against the standards the field expects for AI research (TRIPOD+AI, STARD-AI, CLAIM, CONSORT-AI)

Research areas: oral oncology, molecular diagnostics, oral cancer biomarkers, and artificial intelligence in oncology, across 23 indexed publications.

Data governance

Health data governance in India is not one rulebook. Research conduct is governed by ICMR's national ethical guidelines; personal data by the Digital Personal Data Protection Act; and AI in biomedical research by ICMR's dedicated ethical guidelines for AI in biomedical research and healthcare. Institutional practice has to satisfy all three at once.

  • Institutional Ethics Committee review: assessing research protocols for participant protection, risk, and the adequacy of informed consent
  • Informed consent for data use: including the harder question of secondary use and data reuse, where consent obtained for one study rarely covers the next
  • Research data stewardship: handling of patient and biospecimen data through collection, anonymisation, retention and archival
  • Provenance and traceability: knowing where a sample and its record came from, and being able to evidence it
  • Documented process: ISO coordination at departmental level: written procedures, controlled records, and an audit cycle that tests them

AI governance

The frameworks now converging on healthcare AI (ICMR's AI ethics guidelines, ISO/IEC 42001 for AI management systems, and the reporting standards the journals enforce) ask organisations for something they mostly already know how to do in other domains: define principles, assign accountability, document decisions, and prove it to an auditor.

That is the same shape of work as NAAC accreditation and ISO compliance. Different subject matter, identical machinery: criteria, evidence, internal review, external audit, corrective action. Four years as IQAC–NAAC Coordinator is four years of running exactly that loop across an institution.

  • Principles into criteria: turning fairness, transparency and accountability from statements into things that can be checked
  • Evidence frameworks: building the documentary trail an external assessor will ask for, before they ask
  • Committee governance: chairing and contributing to review bodies that make binding decisions about research
  • Risk framing for clinical deployment: where a model sits in the diagnostic pathway determines how much harm an error can do
  • Bridging technical and clinical stakeholders: eleven years of teaching is eleven years of making difficult material land with a non-expert audience

Direction of travel

The reason this page exists: diagnostic AI is moving from research into deployment, and the constraint is shifting from model performance to whether anyone can demonstrate the thing was built and governed responsibly. That demands people who understand both the clinical ground truth and the audit apparatus. There are not many of us.

I am actively extending this in three directions: formal grounding in AI management system standards, deeper engagement with AI-specific reporting guidelines in my own studies, and advisory work with teams building diagnostic tools.
